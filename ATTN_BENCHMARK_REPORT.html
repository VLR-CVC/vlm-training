Attention benchmark · JUPITER GH200

Why QSA Lost

Qwen Sparse Attention measured 18–59% slower than dense varlen across every arm, dataset and node count. The kernel is not the problem — the untrained indexer is.

Model Qwen3.5-9B · 9.41 B params Scale 16 → 64 nodes · tp=4 · FSDP Steps 40 · random init Date 2026-09-14
01

Verdict

QSA is slower than dense attention on every configuration measured. Two datasets, four architecture variants, three node counts — the sparse arms lose by 18% to 59%, and the gap widens with scale rather than closing.

But the benchmark does not measure what it was built to measure. Every ablation config sets random_init = true, which leaves the frozen QSA indexer holding random weights. A random indexer selects key blocks independently for each query — and that is the one access pattern a block-sparse kernel cannot exploit.

Correction to an earlier call

Freezing the indexer was justified on the grounds that an untrained indexer produces an "arbitrary but fixed" sparsity pattern, which is fine for a throughput benchmark. That reasoning was wrong. Arbitrary is not neutral here — it is the worst case, and it costs roughly 5× against a pattern with the same per-query density.

04

The union is what the kernel sees

A BlockMask is sparse over (query-tile, key-tile) pairs, and a query tile spans 128 queries. Those 128 queries share one row of the mask, so the kernel must visit the union of everything they select. Per-query sparsity is not what gets skipped.

Measured on the mask directly, independent of any kernel timing.

A random indexer visits 98.4% of all causal tiles — the kernel runs fully dense and pays per-element mask evaluation on top. That is the measured 4.8×, explained without appeal to kernel quality.

The corollary matters more than the verdict: with clustered selection flex_attention delivers 0.24–0.31× dense, a 3–4× speedup, which is roughly what QSA promises. The kernel works. The benchmark fed it noise.

06

Findings that stand on their own

The sequence-length axis is gated by vocabulary, not attention

Two separate configurations died on the same allocation, and neither was an attention working set:

clevr  @ 24576   OOM: tried to allocate 11.37 GiB   = 24576 × 248320 × 2 B
plotqa @ 16384   OOM: tried to allocate  7.58 GiB   = 16384 × 248320 × 2 B

model.py:1348 materializes the full logits tensor before the loss ever sees it. Chunked CE chunks the fp32 upcast inside the loss — worth 18 GiB historically — but the bf16 logits already exist by then. With a 248,320-token vocabulary that is the ceiling on seq_len, which is the axis sparse attention lives on.

GatedDeltaNet scales worse than plain full attention

On clevr the dense pair inverts with node count: hybrid wins at 16 nodes (1.531 vs 1.685) and loses by 64 (2.171 vs 2.023), holding 70.5% weak-scaling efficiency against fullattn's 83.3%. Unrelated to QSA, and not something the earlier baseline work surfaced.

07

What would answer the question

required

Measure QSA with a correlated selection pattern. The indexer needs either trained weights or a locality prior. Until then every QSA number here is a lower bound that describes an untrained indexer, not the architecture.

required

Quantify how clustered a trained indexer actually is. The whole result turns on the union statistic. It is cheap to measure on any real checkpoint and decides whether QSA can pay off at all on this kernel.

blocked

Fused linear cross-entropy. Never materialize full logits; project and reduce per chunk. This unblocks 24576 and beyond, and nothing else moves the sequence-length axis.

open

The 128- and 256-node rungs. Not run. Given QSA loses at 64 and widens, these cost real allocation to confirm a trend already visible.

open

Inference benchmark at high seqlen. Scoped in the plan, not started. Decode has one query per step, so the union pathology does not apply — QSA may behave completely differently there.
